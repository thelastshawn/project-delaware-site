The clouds came a week after the release, and they never left.

The scientists who were still alive to say so said the spores were riding the jet stream, high and east, seeding the overcast as they went. Nobody needed a scientist to know the light had changed. It came down flat and grey and a little green, and it stayed that way, summer and winter, for five years.

The sun is still up there. You can see where it is at noon, a paler patch in the ceiling. You cannot see it set. The day just thins, the way a lamp runs out of oil, until it is gone.

And then it is dark. Not city dark, not country dark. *Dark.* No moon, no stars, no glow on the horizon from a town that isn't there any more. The kind of dark people had forgotten existed, the kind the old stories were written about.

---

The first winter killed more people than the strain did. Then the roads went quiet. Then the towns.

Five years on, the land is doing what land does when people leave it. Chaparral has crept back over the shoulders and the parking lots. Lawns that someone once watered are dust and weeds. The fires that nobody fought left black scars across whole valleys. The asphalt is still there, sun-bleached and cracked, with sand drifted against the barriers and the wrecks still sitting where the end caught them.

Nothing grows on the open road. People notice that, and don't like to say why.
